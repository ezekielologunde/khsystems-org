# Learn articles: minor review notes (for a clinician reviewer)
Articles were AI-drafted and fact-checked in three rounds on 2026-11-09. No material issues remained; these minor notes were left for a human reviewer.

## Recent Research on Outpatient Therapy and Care (`whats-new-in-outpatient-therapy-and-care`)
- Swedish study: the article labels the 18% (16 of 89) group 'traditional psychotherapy'. The source limits this to face-to-face CBT, CT and PDT, excluding iCBT. Across all psychological treatments including iCBT the source reports 71.6%. Consider 'face-to-face psychotherapy (CBT, CT or PDT)'.
- Concert Health: the article says more contacts and more days enrolled were 'linked to higher odds of improving'. The paper's outcome was improved or unchanged versus regression (days enrolled OR 1.432, touchpoints OR 2.584). Consider 'higher odds of improving or staying the same rather than getting worse'. The claim that improvement rates across contact-count groups 'did not rise in a straight line' was not checked, because the fetch did not cover the last ~6.6k characters of the page.
- DaylightRx: the paper defines the response as a CGI-I score of 2 or lower and labels only 1 as 'very much improved'. The article's gloss 'much or very much improved' is a conventional paraphrase, not the paper's wording. The 'adjunct to usual care' wording comes from the paper's FDA clearance note, not the trial design description. The article presents this as how the app is 'described', which is acceptable.
- AFP editorial: it says clinicians can assess the 'potential net benefit' of implementing components. The article says 'likely benefit', a close paraphrase. 'Net benefit' would be more exact.
- Cureus review: the source calls telemedicine 'a promising alternative' (conclusions) and 'a viable alternative' (abstract), with caution on equivalence. The article's quote is accurate. The review's other internal inconsistencies (search counts, 9 of 10 versus 8 of 10 low risk of bias, overstated Turner non-inferiority and Comer superiority) are flagged only generally as 'reporting inconsistencies'. This is adequately hedged.
- Video 3YVjvDTFqiI: the oEmbed title 'Types of Telehealth Services' and author HRSA match the article. The Swedish study and Concert Health pages were only read up to about 100k characters, but all the checked claims were confirmed within that range.

## Children and Teen Mental Health Trends: What Recent Data and Research Show (`children-and-teen-mental-health-trends`)
- The YouTube Official Blog source (blog.youtube) is live but does not mention the specific APA video; it only describes an APA partnership and an unnamed video series. - The SickKids news page gives 83,000 youth in 22 countries, JAACAP Open, modest effects mostly in students with existing symptoms, no clear benefit for externalizing behaviours, and better results with mental health professionals and CBT. All match. The article adds 'one review, so read with care', which is reasonable editorial caution.

## Loneliness, Connection and Community: What the Research Shows (`loneliness-connection-and-community`)
- NIHR SPCR source: the page only states that author Maria Panagioti was funded by the NIHR (grant 649, School for Primary Care Research). The article's phrase 'funded in part by the NIHR School for Primary Care Research' is a reasonable but slightly broader reading of that. Consider 'with support from' or dropping the funding clause.
- Source title for the University of Michigan poll is paraphrased. The PDF's actual title is 'Mental Health Crisis Support and Older Adults: Perceptions of the 988 Lifeline' (National Poll on Healthy Aging, July 2026). The listed title could be aligned to it.
- The NIHR SPCR source entry has no year, and the page does not show a publication date, so the year could not be confirmed. The article text does not state a year for it either, so nothing is wrong.
- The meta-analysis effect sizes are small for social functioning (SMD 0.18) and quality of life (0.14), and small-to-moderate for depression (-0.28) and anxiety (-0.47). 'Small to moderate' is accurate overall.
- The CMI report's 'more than three-quarters positive view' is a headline statement. The supporting figures in the report are 87% of parents and 72% of young adults agreeing treatment would be beneficial. The article's wording matches the report.
- The CMI report also notes the survey's stigma findings were not always statistically significant. The article's hedging about self-reported attitudes covers this adequately.
- Verified correct: WHO figures (1 in 6; 17-21% ages 13-29; 24% vs 11%; 871,000 deaths and 100 per hour; isolation data more limited; roadmap; everyday steps; June 30, 2025). NORC figures (n=1,090; Nov 6-10, 2025; 10% very/extremely familiar; about 60% limited/no familiarity; 62% vs 39%; margin of error 4 points). University of Michigan figures (n=2,379; ages 50-95; September 2025; July 2026; 69% never heard; 4% contacted; free, confidential, 24/7). Umbrella review (35 reviews; paid peer support; 97% low or critically low quality; mixed results; depression, self-efficacy and recovery). CMI/Ipsos figures (1,000 parents; 500 young adults ages 18-24; July 30 to August 22, 2024; all percentages match). YouTube video LpSDuDIaBGk matches the title and channel (Kasley Killam, TED).

## Recovery, Rehabilitation and Peer Support: What Recent Research Shows (`recovery-rehabilitation-and-peer-support`)
- Garbled phrase in the supported employment section: "(AMSR 2 is the name of the tool: AMSTAR 2)". It reads like an editing artifact. Replace with "(the tool is called AMSTAR 2)".
- The 1.92 figure (95% CI 1.53 to 2.42) is an odds ratio in the Radu et al. umbrella review (Hellstrom et al.), though the review's own text calls it "1.92 more likely". The article's "1.92 times more likely" repeats that loose wording. Saying "odds 1.92 times higher" would be more precise. The 2.4 figure (CI 1.99 to 2.90, Modini et al.) and the 51% vs 18% figure (Twamley et al.) match the source. The source describes 18% as the control group, which the article calls traditional vocational rehabilitation. That is consistent with Twamley's comparison, but I could not confirm it from the truncated page text.
- Negative symptoms: the article says "commonly identified barrier" and that this "rests mainly on one meta-analysis" with small to moderate effects. The Radu abstract calls it "the most robustly identified barrier", and the part of the paper I could read gives small-to-moderate effects for the Tse et al. meta-analysis. The "rests mainly on one meta-analysis" claim is a simplification I could not fully confirm, because the second half of the paper was truncated in my fetch. Consider softening it, or noting that the source calls it the most robustly identified barrier.
- The article says the paper's text and table disagree about at least one review's rating. This is confirmed: the text says no review is high quality, while Table 3 rates Kinoshita et al. "High". The count "most of 35 rated critically low or low" is supported (about 28 critically low, 6 low, 1 high).
- The Springer scoping review page blocked direct fetching. I verified its details through a search summary of the abstract instead: published 16 February 2026, 17 articles retained, definitions heterogeneous, framed by psychosocial rehabilitation and personal recovery. The article's statement that the review did not measure effectiveness matches the abstract's stated aim, but I could not read the full text.
- The APPEAR source (Hackman et al., BMJ Open 15:e105211) matches the authors, the five conditions and the six recovery areas. The source itself is inconsistent on whether it synthesised 18 or 20 studies. The article does not state a number, so there is no issue.
- The Birmingham PDF says it was prepared by Maria Teresa Ferazzoli (August 2024). It states the CHIME framework, that recovery is not linked to eliminating symptoms, that the person defines recovery, that the evidence review points to positive implications for people using services and for practitioners, and that it is not an exhaustive review. The "IMPACT Network" and "University of Birmingham" labels come from the URL domain (impact.bham.ac.uk) and are not fully visible in the extracted text. This is not a problem.
- Digital peer support (Croke, Tyler et al., DOI 10.1136/bmjment-2025-302275): all figures match the NIHR page. These include 29 studies, 5,825 participants, depression -0.28, anxiety -0.47, small gains in social functioning, quality of life and personal recovery, patient activation, no significant effect on engagement or satisfaction, preliminary evidence benefits persist, and "may be considered as an adjunct to usual care".
- Yeo et al. (Epidemiology and Psychiatric Sciences, 13 February 2025): 73 mental health and 47 physical health studies are correct. The paper targeted healthy individuals in general populations, with results similar across varying degrees of existing health conditions. Informal support had an SMD of 0.74, against 0.37 for formal peer support. Apps (0.42) and video conferencing (0.41) were above forums, websites and email. Mental health effects weakened at follow-up (0.54 to 0.37). Heterogeneity was very high, and many studies bundled other components such as psychoeducation. The video-conferencing confidence interval is very wide, which supports the article's "uncertain" wording. All consistent with the article.
- The video id 6o4cwCPUqLY matches: oEmbed returns the title "The Power of Peers in Recovery" and the author NAMI-NYC, as listed.

## Getting Mental Health Care with Maryland Medicaid: What to Know (`getting-care-with-maryland-medicaid`)
- 988 statistic: KFF says data as of March 2026 show volume of calls, texts and chats 15% higher than a year earlier. It does not say this is a single-month comparison. The article's 'in March 2026 ... was 15 percent higher' could be softened to 'as of March 2026'.
- The article says the H.R. 1 deck 'was made for community health workers'. The deck text does not say so. The inference comes only from the URL folder (Community-Health-Workers). Consider 'posted on MDH's community health worker page'.
- 'Carelon Behavioral Health runs Maryland's public behavioral health system': the MDH page and the report describe Carelon as the behavioral health administrative services organization (ASO), which processes claims and handles provider and member access. 'Administers' or 'is the administrative services organization for' would be more precise than 'runs'.
- Federal rule date: KFF says CMS issued the interim final rule on June 1, 2026. The Maryland AG release says June 3, 2026. The article only says 'June 2026', which is safe. Keep it non-specific.
- The 'no referral or authorization requirement described' statement is correctly hedged to the pages used. Carelon's Maryland page does link to service authorization grids, so some services may need authorization. The existing advice to call the clinic or the HealthChoice Help Line covers this.
- The telehealth guide is dated 2025-05-15, and the PDF file metadata shows April 2026. The article's date is correct per the document text, and it already says policies may have changed.
- The pause release says the extension runs from July 1, 2026 for six months and gives no exact end date. The article matches this wording.
- Verified with no issues: the Optum contract (1/1/2020 to 12/31/2024), claims moving to Carelon on 12/21-22/2024, and Carelon claims processing from 1/1/2025. The HealthChoice Help Line number 800-284-4510. The PT 13-26 details (dated Aug 21, 2025; NPI on PRP claims from Jan 1, 2026; denial if the referrer is not enrolled; the three COMAR referral criteria). The ten-area pause and four program types. The deck dates (Oct 1, 2026; Jan 1, 2027; retroactive coverage one month for expansion adults and two for others; redeterminations listed as both Jan 2027 and July 2027; disabling mental disorders in the frailty list). KFF calling the frailty definition 'restrictive'. The AG suit on June 29, 2026 with 24 AGs and two governors. The YouTube video mTI_MjHFWW4 matches the title and the NAMI channel.
